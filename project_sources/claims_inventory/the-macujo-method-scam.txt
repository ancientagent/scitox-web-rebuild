Aloe, Macujo & Toxin Shampoos - History & deception

History of Aloe Rid and Macujo Method
The Macujo Method dates back over 20 years to one of the first online drug discussion forums. It was created by someone trying to pass a THC hair follicle test, who threw together household items like salicylic acid face wash, Tide detergent, baking soda, apple cider vinegar, and Nexxus Aloe Rid shampoo. Over the years, user reports have been mixed, with about 80% saying it didn't work and 20% claiming success. However, many of those reporting success often had lighter usage histories.

The Macujo Method and Jerry G Method (another made up method thought up by a forum) are often promoted by companies selling variations of Nexxus Aloe Rid or similar products with names like Aloe, Macujo, Toxin, or Rid, as well as Zydot. This is because they can't profit from a method that's freely available online. However, these shampoos are essentially overpriced regular shampoos. A quick look at the ingredients list compared to other shampoos reveals there's no difference, making them a waste of money.

You can achieve the same results using the Macujo or Jerry G Method with or without these shampoos. Dozens of repetitions of scrubbing household products on your scalp has a tendency to remove some toxins, although generally not enough for most people to pass and more than enough for most people to experieicne painful sores and significant hair damage. Websites selling these Aloe shampoos often engage in unethical and deceptive practices, with fake reviews, dubious claims, and guarantees, not to mention numerous BBB complaints.

They claim propylene glycol is the "magic" ingredient, but it's a common substance found in many everyday items, including cosmetics, dog food, and vape juice.Instead of relying on a bunch of random household items and a process developed in a day by someone that was stoned you might consider products that are scientifically formulated, tested, and proven in thousands of experiments to pass a hair follicle test.

Zydot Ultra Clean
A study published in the Int J Legal Med. revealed that Zydot Ultra Clean removed as little as 5% of metabolites. The study stated, "All drugs originally present in the hair fibers were still detected after a single application of Ultra Clean. However, a slight decrease in drug concentrations could mostly be observed... The findings clearly demonstrated that drug substances had not been sufficiently removed by Zydot."

In summary, all test subjects still failed after using Zydot.

Why are these the main products/methods showing up on Google?

It's called affiliate marketing. They pay for a popular website to post a review article about their products with links to their website. When victims fall for the fake review and click on the link to the detox website, the website where the article is posted gets a fat commission. Usually between $40-$60. This is the same thing for YouTube or pretty much anything else where you have an influencer of sort that promotes a product and gets a commission when a customer they referred buys the product through the link.

This is why there are countless websites praising these companies and products, even though they’ve often never actually tried them. Here’s a straight-shooting roundup of what people actually say online about Livity , Hair Razor , and Zydot Ultra Clean for passing a hair-follicle drug test—pulled from Trustpilot (where available), Amazon, Reddit, and other independent sources.

Zydot Ultra Clean

Where reviews exist: Amazon has the richest set. Trustpilot doesn’t have a robust product page for Zydot itself.

What users report (patterns):

Split verdicts : Amazon has both “ I passed ” stories and “ I failed even after following directions” stories; many successful reviewers say they used multiple boxes and/or combined with other routines (e.g., vinegar/baking soda or DIY methods). Amazon+1

Plenty of fails : there are clear “failed after weeks of trying Zydot/Aloe Rid/Macujo” reviews as well. Amazon

Overall read: Inconsistent . Some passes (often with multiple boxes + adjunct methods ), but many fails—especially when used alone .

Hair Razor

Where reviews exist: Couldn’t locate a stand-alone Trustpilot page; there are forum posts and a Sitejabber page for a reseller (“passitkit”) showing a small number of reviews (4★ avg across 8). Treat reseller ratings cautiously. SiteJabber

What users report (patterns):

Extremely time-intensive : common Reddit refrain is needing dozens to hundreds of washes; some say they don’t have time for what Hair Razor seems to require. Reddit

Mixed results : scattered success claims vs. multiple reports that it didn’t move the needle (especially without massive repetition). Some posts compare it unfavorably to other options. Reddit

Overall read: High effort, variable results. Most independent chatter frames Hair Razor as a grind (many washes), with outcomes all over the place.

Livity

Where reviews exist: No dedicated Trustpilot page I could find; feedback shows up mostly on Amazon listings and Reddit threads. An Amazon “Procleanse Hair Clarifying System” listing exists (branding overlaps with “Livity/ProCleanse”). Amazon

What users report (patterns):

Very heavy repetition : multiple Redditers describe doing 6–8+ cycles , sometimes 20–30+ cycles over ~1–2 weeks to feel confident. One post: “I did it 28 times ,” ~30 minutes each cycle. Reddit

Mixed outcomes : some success anecdotes, others say effort/time is intense with no guarantee. A separate thread mentions pairing methods or considering alternatives depending on hair type. Reddit

Overall read: Reviews are sparse and anecdotal . Reported “wins” tend to involve many cycles and other tactics; there’s no independent lab data showing reliable pass rates. When it does appear to work it is with very light usage. Any moderate or heavy usage feedback indicates failure.

Bottom line

All three have anecdotal “it worked for me” posts and “it failed” posts.

The more repetition + more add-ons a product requires (many cycles, multiple boxes, vinegar/baking soda, etc.), the less reliable it tends to look in independent chatter.

None of these have strong, transparent evidence or consistent general consensus demonstrating consistent pass rates for forensic hair testing.
